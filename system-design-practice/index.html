Why reading solutions is not enough

Reading a finished design builds recognition: you see a diagram and think it looks right. An interview, and real design work, tests recall. You start from a blank page, an ambiguous prompt, and a clock. The gap between those two skills is why people who have read many solutions still freeze on the first question.

Deliberate practice closes that gap. It means working on one specific skill, producing your own answer before looking at anyone else's, getting concrete feedback, and repeating the weak area until it stops being weak.

Pick one narrow skill per session

The most common practice mistake is choosing the largest, most famous problem every time. A problem that touches ten concerns gives you feedback on none of them. Instead, choose a prompt that isolates the skill you want to improve, then match it to a problem.

Skill to isolateWhat the problem forces you to decide
Caching and invalidationWhat to cache, where, for how long, and how stale entries are handled (a distributed cache)
Rate limiting and fairnessAlgorithm choice, shared counters, behavior under burst (an API rate limiter)
Asynchronous workQueues, retries, delivery guarantees, fan-out (a notification system)
Scheduling and reliabilityExactly-once versus at-least-once, leases, recovery (a job scheduler)
Consistency under contentionPreventing double booking and handling concurrent updates (a hotel booking system)
Correctness and idempotencyRetries, duplicate requests, reconciliation (a payment system)

A 60-minute practice session

A fixed structure keeps sessions comparable, so you can tell whether you are actually improving. This is a workable template; shorten or lengthen the blocks to fit your schedule.

BlockTimeWhat you do
Read the prompt5 minRead once, then close any reference material
Write requirements10 minFunctional requirements, non-goals, scale assumptions, success criteria
Build and annotate25 minDraw the architecture and label each flow and component with why it exists
Review10 minCheck the design against your own requirements and the checklist below, writing down weaknesses without fixing them yet
Revise one thing10 minChange the design to address the single most important weakness

Writing down weaknesses before editing the diagram matters. If you fix problems as you notice them, you never see which kinds of mistakes you repeat.

A self-review checklist

After building, review your own design before comparing it with any reference. Ask these questions about each major component and flow:

  • Does every component trace back to a stated requirement or a number you estimated?
  • Can I explain what this component costs, such as latency, consistency, operational burden, or money?
  • What is the single point of failure, and what happens when it fails?
  • Where does the data live, which copy is the source of truth, and how do the others stay in sync?
  • What happens at ten times the assumed load? Which part breaks first?
  • Is each operation safe to retry, or can a retry cause a duplicate effect?
  • How would I notice that this is broken in production?

Keep a decision log

A diagram records what you built. A decision log records why, which is the part interviewers and teammates actually test. For each significant choice, write four short lines:

  • Decision: what you chose.
  • Alternatives: what you considered and rejected.
  • Reason and cost: why you chose it and what it gives up.
  • Revisit when: the condition, such as a scale threshold, that would change the decision.

Reading your own log a week later is one of the fastest ways to spot shallow reasoning, such as decisions that have no stated alternative or no stated cost.

A four-week plan

If you have a month, group practice by theme rather than by company or product. Each week deepens one layer, and the final week combines them under time pressure.

Common mistakes in practice

  • Memorizing diagrams. A memorized answer collapses as soon as the interviewer changes one requirement.
  • Always practicing the same famous problem and never the ones that expose your gaps.
  • Skipping the review step, so each session repeats the last session's mistakes.
  • Practicing only the area you already enjoy, such as drawing boxes, and avoiding estimation or failure analysis.
  • Never timing yourself, then running out of time in the real interview.
  • Practicing silently. Explaining your reasoning out loud, or to another person, is a separate skill that needs its own repetition.

How to tell you are improving

Progress in system design is easy to feel and hard to measure, so use concrete signals. You can produce a usable estimate in about five minutes. You can name at least two trade-offs for each major component without prompting. You find your own weaknesses during review instead of discovering them later.

One of the most useful tests is to repeat a problem two weeks later without looking at your earlier answer. If the second attempt is clearer, better justified, and faster, the practice is working.

Practice with another person

Solo practice builds the design skill, but a partner builds the conversation skill. Ask someone to play the interviewer: they give the prompt, answer clarifying questions, and push back on one decision. Even a friend who is not a system design expert can ask why you chose something, which forces you to articulate reasoning you may have only half formed.

When no partner is available, record yourself explaining the design and listen for places where you skip a reason or hedge a decision.

Frequently asked questions

How can I practice system design on my own?

Pick a problem, write requirements first, draw the architecture, explain why each component exists, and review the result before changing the diagram.

What is deliberate practice in system design?

It means isolating one skill, such as caching or database scaling, getting feedback on it, and repeating until the weakness disappears, instead of only solving new problems.

How do I know my design is good?

A good design meets the stated requirements, makes its trade-offs explicit, and survives walkthroughs of overload, partial failure, and recovery.
